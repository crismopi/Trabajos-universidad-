L238@P9: Executive summary
L239@P9: This World Bank Systematic Country Diagnostic (SCD) provides an
L240@P9: analytical foundation for identifying country-level actions and
L241@P9: investments that would increase growth and boost shared prosperity in Kenya. It aims to be an objective diagnostic that
L242@P9: analyzes and prioritizes binding constraints on development, based on government and external sources,
L243@P9: as well as consultations with local stakeholders.
L244@P9: The conceptual framework adopted for this SCD is organized around
L245@P9: two main strategies: (1) higher aggregate output and productivity growth and (2) greater equity and inclusiveness. Constraints
L246@P9: limiting advancement in these two key thematic areas
L247@P9: are identified using the latest available data. The document then outlines three main pathways to accelerate
L248@P9: the attainment of the World Bank’s “twin goals” of ending extreme poverty and promoting shared prosperity.
L249@P9: Using filtering criteria and with attention to foundational
L250@P9: issues that cut across all sectors, this SCD proposes specific priorities for development initiatives in Kenya.

L287@P10: EXECUTIVE
L288@P10: SUMMARY 2005/06 to 36.1 percent in 2015/16 and was projected
L289@P10: to decline to 33.5 percent in 2019. Although robust
L290@P10: gross domestic product (GDP) growth over the past
L291@P10: decade has contributed to the decline in poverty, at
L292@P10: the current pace of poverty reduction, this will not
L293@P10: be enough to eradicate extreme poverty by 2030. To
L294@P10: do so, Kenya’s poverty rate would have to fall by an
L295@P10: unrealistic 6.1 percentage points each year, while the
L296@P10: country’s decade average is just 1.6 percentage points
L297@P10: per year. If poverty reduction continues at its current
L298@P10: pace, the poverty rate will remain around 24 percent in
L299@P10: 2030—or higher—given the likely negative effects of
L300@P10: COVID-19 on poverty.
L301@P10: Kenya has seen a return to strong growth
L302@P10: rates, but the increased public spending
L303@P10: that has helped drive that expansion will
L304@P10: not be sustainable over the long term.
L305@P10: The Kenyan economy has emerged from an economic slump and
L306@P10: returned to robust, resilient growth over the past decade and a
L307@P10: half. Kenya’s post-independence economic growth is
L308@P10: characterized by three broadly distinct phases: buoyant growth in the post-independence period, an economic slump in the 1980s and 1990s, and then a robust
L309@P10: rebound. Since 2004, GDP growth has rebounded
L310@P10: to an average of 5.4 percent and remained resilient
L311@P10: during and in the aftermath of the global financial
L312@P10: crisis. Despite performing better than the average for
L313@P10: Sub-Saharan Africa, Kenya’s growth remains weaker
L314@P10: than a number of its regional and structural peers.
L315@P10: The ongoing COVID-19 pandemic poses a significant
L316@P10: threat to near-term growth in Kenya because of the
L317@P10: contraction of domestic demand, a decline in exports,
L318@P10: the disruption of global supply chains, and a drop in
L319@P10: tourism revenue.
L320@P10: The recent recovery in growth has been broad based but driven primarily by the services sector on the supply side and by an increase in
L321@P10: domestic demand; the debt-driven, public investment–led growth
L322@P10: model seems to have reached its limit. The services sector
L323@P10: accounted for some three-quarters of the increase
L324@P10: in GDP growth, while the agricultural sector contributed the least. The increase in private consumption
L325@P10: and investment were the main drivers of the increase
L326@P10: in domestic demand. Increased government spending has also been a driver of growth in the post-2012
L327@P10: era. Overall, total expenditure by the national government increased to an average of 26.6 percent of GDP
L328@P10: between 2013/14 and 2017/18, compared with an
L329@P10: average of 23.4 percent in the preceding four years.
L330@P10: The public sector is however unlikely to remain a
L331@P10: driver of growth, given a narrowing of the fiscal space
L332@P10: due to an underperformance in revenues.
L333@P10: Factor accumulation has been the greatest driver of the recent
L334@P10: pickup in growth, whereas productivity growth significantly lags
L335@P10: that of comparator countries. The bulk of the increase in
L336@P10: GDP growth in recent decades has come from capital deepening and labor force growth. Although the
L337@P10: contribution of productivity to GDP growth in Kenya
L338@P10: has picked up from the period of economic slump, it
L339@P10: still lags significantly behind that of comparator countries. This suggests there is room for greater growth by
L340@P10: addressing the challenges to low productivity in the
L341@P10: Kenyan economy.
L342@P10: The pace of structural reforms has increased since the mid-2000s,
L343@P10: and these have helped boost productivity growth, but there remains
L344@P10: further room for improvement. The single most important
L345@P10: determinant of the changes in per capita GDP growth
L346@P10: in Kenya over the long term has been the pace of structural reforms, meaning changes in government policies, practices, and institutions that can help create an
L347@P10: enabling environment for growth and job creation. In
L348@P10: the 1980s, state controls were maintained in several
L349@P10: important markets, including a pegged exchange rate,
L350@P10: interest rate controls, price controls, and the licensing of foreign exchange transactions. A lack of structural reforms contributed to the economic slump that
L351@P10: occurred from 1981 to 2003. Successive national governments were able to accelerate the pace of structural
L352@P10: reforms after a political shift in 2003. These reforms
L353@P10: included restoring macro stability, strengthening governance institutions, and expanding physical infrastructure. From a very low contribution in the 1980s
L354@P10: and 1990s, the contribution of structural reforms to
L355@P10: growth quadrupled in the 2000s. Notwithstanding this
L356@P10: progress, the contribution of structural reforms to per
L357@P10: capita GDP growth in Kenya still lags that of its aspirational peers.
L358@P10: Kenya is still facing a range of macroeconomic
L359@P10: and microeconomic constraints that are
L360@P10: preventing productivity growth.
L361@P10: Sustained increases in productivity are critical to achievement of
L362@P10: the twin goals. The recent pickup in productivity growth
L363@P10: has been predominantly driven by improvements in
L364@P10: productivity within firms and farms, with the contribution from dynamic reallocation (entry of new firms and
L365@P10: rearranging the factors of production) being minimal.
L366@P10: Productivity growth is largely coming from incumbents within the same sector, with little room for new
L367@P10: players, a phenomenon that could reflect challenges
L368@P10: due to barriers to entry.
L369@P10: Narrowing of the fiscal space poses the single biggest threat to
L370@P10: macroeconomic stability. Debt-to-GDP ratios surged from
L371@P10: 39 percent of GDP in 2013 to 62 percent of GDP in 2019,
L372@P10: with the composition of that debt shifting toward
L373@P10: more expensive commercial sources. The expansionary fiscal stance, coupled with the interest rate cap,
