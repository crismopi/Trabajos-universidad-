L166@P8: EXECUTIVE SUMMARY
L167@P8: Kenya managed to contain the health and economic
L168@P8: impacts of multiple COVID-19 waves in 2021, helped
L169@P8: by targeted containment measures and progress
L170@P8: on vaccination, but is now facing a potentially large
L171@P8: economic shock from the war in Ukraine.
L172@P8: Kenya’s economy has staged a remarkable recovery from
L173@P8: the worst economic effects of the pandemic. Real GDP
L174@P8: increased by 7.5 percent in 2021, higher than the estimated
L175@P8: growth in Sub-Saharan Africa of 4 percent. This growth was
L176@P8: driven by the recovery of the services sector and expansion
L177@P8: in industrial output. By contrast, the agriculture sector’s
L178@P8: output contracted by 0.2 percent in 2021, affected by
L179@P8: drought conditions in the arid and semi-arid lands (ASAL).
L180@P8: Kenya’s economic performance remained strong in
L181@P8: the early months of 2022 but external challenges have
L182@P8: mounted. Kenya’s exposure to the war in Ukraine through
L183@P8: direct trade linkages is moderate, with Russia and Ukraine
L184@P8: accounting for only 2.1 percent of total goods trade
L185@P8: between 2015 and 2020. Similarly, tourists from Ukraine
L186@P8: and Russia do not account for a significant share of Kenya’s
L187@P8: tourism market. However, the economy is vulnerable
L188@P8: to the commodity price shocks resulting from the war,
L189@P8: particularly through fuel, fertilizer, wheat and other food
L190@P8: imports. Global financial conditions have also tightened
L191@P8: sharply, increasing external financing costs.
L192@P8: The Central Bank of Kenya reoriented its accommodative
L193@P8: monetary policy in view of elevated risks to the inflation
L194@P8: outlook. The Central Bank Rate has been raised by 50
L195@P8: basis points (bps) to 7.5 percent in response to emerging
L196@P8: inflationary pressures from the surge in global commodity
L197@P8: prices and supply chain disruptions. Headline inflation
L198@P8: eased from Q4 2021 through February 2022 (5.1 percent
L199@P8: y/y), helped by a 15 percent electricity tariff cut. However,
L200@P8: inflation subsequently increased sharply to 7.1 percent
L201@P8: y/y in May 2022 as domestic food prices, and fuel prices
L202@P8: (in March to May), increased following the surge in global
L203@P8: commodity prices due to the war in Ukraine. The impact
L204@P8: of the global oil price shock on domestic prices has been
L205@P8: cushioned by government subsidies.
L206@P8: A strong recovery in revenues has supported fiscal
L207@P8: performance but this is now being countered by the
L208@P8: cost of subsidizing fuel. The rebound in economic activity
L209@P8: and ongoing tax reforms and revenue administration
L210@P8: improvements have boosted revenue collection. Revenue
L211@P8: in the current fiscal year through Q3 remained on target and
L212@P8: performed above the previous year’s outturn (12.3 percent
L213@P8: of GDP in Q3 2021/22 against a target of 11.2 percent of
L214@P8: GDP in Q3 2020/21). As a result, the fiscal deficit in Q3
L215@P8: FY2021/22 shrank to 3.9 percent of full-year GDP from 4.4
L216@P8: percent a year earlier. However, the limited passthrough of
L217@P8: higher international oil prices to consumers is generating
L218@P8: fiscal costs, with the total monthly cost of subsidizing fuel
L219@P8: estimated to be approximately US$66 million.
L220@P8: Looking ahead, economic growth is expected to
L221@P8: moderate in 2022 with real GDP projected to grow by 5.5
L222@P8: percent in 2022 and 5.2 percent on average in 2023–24.
L223@P8: Offsetting the continued recovery from the pandemic is
L224@P8: the impact of the war in Ukraine, which has clouded the
L225@P8: outlook for the global economic recovery. Domestically,
L226@P8: a key risk to the outlook is a further worsening of the
L227@P8: current drought, which is having a devastating effect
L228@P8: on food security and livelihoods in affected parts
L229@P8: of the country and is necessitating increased social
L230@P8: spending on food assistance. For example, using the
L231@P8: Integrated Food Security Phase Classification (IPC), it is
L232@P8: estimated that 3.1 million Kenyans (out of 13.6 million)
L233@P8: living in ASAL counties are food insecure. The baseline
L234@P8: projections assume that the country will receive below
L235@P8: average rains that will negatively affect agricultural
L236@P8: performance and also account for the downside effects
L237@P8: of the ongoing war in Ukraine through increased global
L238@P8: commodity prices.
L239@P8: One sector is critical to achieving Kenya’s development
L240@P8: goals, accounts for a large share of government
L241@P8: spending, and was hit hard by the COVID-19 pandemic:
L242@P8: education. Accordingly, the special topic of this Kenya
L243@P8: Economic Update (KEU) is the performance and financing
L244@P8: of the education sector, drawing on a forthcoming World
L245@P8: Bank Public Expenditure Review (PER) on education. It
L246@P8: examines the impressive improvements which Kenya
L247@P8: has achieved in education outcomes, the remaining
L248@P8: challenges in the sector including charting a successful
L249@P8: recovery from the pandemic, and how the allocation of
L250@P8-9: resources can contribute to resolving these.iv June 2022 | Edition No. 25
L251@P9: Executive Summary
L252@P9: Kenya has significantly improved education outcomes.
L253@P9: Gross enrollment rates have increased to 78 percent in
L254@P9: early-childhood education and 70 percent in secondary
L255@P9: education (whilst remaining above 100 percent in
L256@P9: primary). Combining access and learning into LearningAdjusted Years of Education (LAYS) shows that Kenya is
L257@P9: above what is expected by its level of income; a child in
L258@P9: Kenya completes, on average, 11.6 years of education,
L259@P9: which, when adjusted by the level of learning relative to
L260@P9: other countries, results in 8.4 effective years of schooling.
L261@P9: This is the highest LAYS in Africa. While Kenya does well for
L262@P9: its level of income, this does not mean that Kenya should
L263@P9: not aim to further improve both access and, especially,
L264@P9: quality of education in order to improve the LAYS and the
L265@P9: World Bank’s Human Capital Index (HCI), which measures
L266@P9: the level of development of human capital in a country.
L267@P9: The HCI has shown strong correlation with both economic
L268@P9: growth and equity.
L269@P9: The COVID-19 pandemic has resulted in learning losses
L270@P9: and deepened inequalities in the education sector.
L271@P9: Around 17 million students and more than 320,000
L272@P9: teachers were affected by the closure of 30,000 primary
L273@P9: and secondary schools during 2020. The partial reopening
L274@P9: of schools began in October 2020, for students in grades 4,
L275@P9: 8 and 12. In January 2021, all basic and tertiary education
L276@P9: institutions fully reopened. Efforts to provide remote
L277@P9: learning revealed a significant digital divide, with over 50
L278@P9: percent of students not being able to engage in remote
L279@P9: learning opportunities mainly due to lack of devices,
L280@P9: electricity and internet connectivity. There was significant
L281@P9: disruption to capital spending and to preparations for the
L282@P9: new Competency Based Curriculum (CBC).
L283@P9: The government now faces the challenge of ensuring
L284@P9: that the pandemic does not leave lasting scars
L285@P9: through its education effects, and to tackle the
L286@P9: remaining medium-term challenges. Key structural
L287@P9: challenges include increasing the enrollment in postprimary education, improving learning outcomes, and
L288@P9: reducing deep inequalities. As Kenya already spends a
L289@P9: comparatively high share of resources on education,
L290@P9: meeting these challenges will require ensuring that
L291@P9: resource allocations remain adequate, and improving
L292@P9: equity and efficiency in the use of resources. In particular,
L293@P9: continuing and accelerating the improvements in the
L294@P9: sector will depend on providing adequate resources
L295@P9: to achieve sector objectives and implement ambitious
L296@P9: reforms; equitable allocation of resources where they
L297@P9: are most needed and are likely to have the most impact;
L298@P9: and efficiency through improvements in management
L299@P9: practices at the school level, improving the management
L300@P9: at the local level and providing extra support for regions
L301@P9: with the most difficulties.
L302@P9: With continued efforts, Kenya can build on the strong
L303@P9: foundations of its education system by continuing to
L304@P9: improve access to and the quality of education, equipping
L305@P9: it to be a driver of growth towards becoming an upper
L306@P9: middle-income country, and reducing inequalities.

L318@P11: 1. Recent Economic Developments and Outlook
L319@P11: 1.1. The global and regional economic
L320@P11: recovery from the pandemic is slowing in
L321@P11: the face of multiple pressures, including
L322@P11: the invasion of Ukraine1
L323@P11: The global economy’s recovery from the COVID-19
L324@P11: pandemic is losing steam as it faces a series of shocks.
L325@P11: The global economy is estimated to have rebounded by 5.5
L326@P11: percent in 2021 and prior to the Russian invasion of Ukraine
L327@P11: was projected to grow by 4.1 percent. Pent-up demand
L328@P11: for goods and constrained supply responses due to the
L329@P11: lingering effects of the pandemic led to a broad-based
L330@P11: increase in the prices of commodities and other input
L331@P11: prices. Given the strong recovery in demand, major central
L332@P11: banks started to gradually withdraw the unprecedented
L333@P11: monetary policy stimulus deployed at the onset of the
L334@P11: pandemic. Russia’s invasion of Ukraine in February 2022
L335@P11: adds new headwinds through rising commodity prices,
L336@P11: tighter financing conditions, trade and travel disruptions,
L337@P11: new movements of migrants and refugees, and generally
L338@P11: increased economic volatility. These forces are weighing on
L339@P11: economic activity and leading to additional inflation and
L340@P11: fiscal pressures, thus posing challenges to policymakers
L341@P11: worldwide. Weighed down by the war in Ukraine and
L342@P11: China’s recent COVID-related lockdowns, global growth is
L343@P11: projected to fall to 3.2 percent in 2022. The global outlook
L344@P11: remains highly uncertain and will depend on the duration
L345@P11: and severity of the war. New coronavirus variants and
L346@P11: outbreaks of COVID-19 also pose continuing risks to global
L347@P11: economic activity.
L348@P11: The economic recovery in Sub-Saharan Africa (SSA)
L349@P11: has decelerated amid high volatility and uncertainty
L350@P11: of external economic and financial conditions. In SubSaharan Africa, many countries’ economies are struggling to
L351@P11: gain momentum amid the slowdown in global economic
L352@P11: activity, continued supply constraints, high inflation, and
L353@P11: rising fiscal risks due to high debt levels. The pace of
L354@P11: economic growth in the region is expected to moderate
L355@P11: in 2022, expanding by 3.6 percent, down from 4 percent
L356@P11: in 2021.
L357@P11: Kenya faces a potentially large economic shock from
L358@P11: the war in Ukraine. Kenya’s exposure through direct
L359@P11: trade linkages appears moderate, with Russia and Ukraine
L360@P11: accounting for 2.1 percent of Kenya’s total goods trade
L361@P11: between 2015 and 2020. Similarly, tourists from Ukraine
L362@P11: and Russia do not account for a significant share of Kenya’s
L363@P11: tourism market. However, the economy is vulnerable to
L364@P11: commodity price shocks resulting from the war in Ukraine.
L365@P11: Kenya is a net oil importer; imports of refined fuel products
L366@P11: amounted to $3.2 billion in 2021 (15% of total imports).
L367@P11: Increased global prices are putting upward pressure on
L368@P11: headline CPI, increasing the fiscal costs of fuel subsidies,
L369@P11: and leading to a deterioration of the current account
L370@P11: balance. The latter is also likely to be adversely affected by
L371@P11: weaker global demand and tourism from advanced and
L372@P11: developing countries. The impact of higher oil prices is
L373@P11: not only limited to a rise in the import bill but would also
L374@P11: extend to increased cost of production and transportation
L375@P11: impacting the utilities, manufacturing and services sectors.
L376@P11: Increased world prices for wheat products (2.3 percent of
L377@P11: total imports) and spillovers to other food products could
L378@P11: have adverse effects on inflation and food security. Higher
L379@P11: prices for fertilizer (1.2 percent of total imports) could
L380@P11: weigh on agricultural output.
L381@P11: 1.2. The Kenyan economy rebounded
L382@P11: in 2021 despite a contraction in
L383@P11: agricultural output
L384@P11: Kenya managed to contain the health and economic
L385@P11: impacts of multiple COVID-19 waves in 2021, helped
L386@P11: by targeted containment measures and progress on
L387@P11: vaccination. The Delta and Omicron COVID-19 variant
