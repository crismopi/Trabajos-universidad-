L91@P8: EXECUTIVE SUMMARY
L92@P8: The colonial period agriculture and rural development policies favoured white settler
L93@P8: agriculture. The indigenous populations were confined in reserve lands mainly providing
L94@P8: labour to settlers. Land policies articulated during this time favoured the settler run largescale agriculture with commodity boards instituted to cater for marketing and regulating
L95@P8: agricultural production. Towards independence (early 1960s) land subdivision consolidation,
L96@P8: registration reallocation and relaxing restrictions on commercial enterprises through the
L97@P8: Swynerton plan permitted the entry of indigenous population into hitherto exclusive and
L98@P8: lucrative agricultural enterprise. The post-independence economic experience in Kenya can
L99@P8: be viewed in three stages: pre-liberalization era (period before 1980), liberalization era
L100@P8: (1980-2002) and post-liberalization era (2003 and the period after)
L101@P8: On gaining independence, the new government inherited a colonial economic that
L102@P8: emphasized import substitution policy propped with overvalued exchange rates. To achieve
L103@P8: macroeconomic stabilization, the government imposed quantitative controls, high tariffs on
L104@P8: competing imports, highly overvalued exchange rates, price and wage regulations, taxation
L105@P8: of exports and requirement of ‘no objection certificates’ from domestic producers. The rapid
L106@P8: economic growth realized during this time was attributed to the inward oriented policies and
L107@P8: stable macroeconomic management. During this period agricultural funding and rural
L108@P8: development was imbalanced with resources directed towards high potential areas and
L109@P8: marginalization of low potential areas. The effects emanating from the first and second oil
L110@P8: shocks and the coffee boom served to destabilize the economic trend.
L111@P8: The introduction of Structural Adjustment Programs (SAPs) in 1980 marked the start of
L112@P8: liberalization in Kenya. The aim of SAPs was to shift the economy from an inward
L113@P8: orientation to externally oriented policies that subject economic agents to competition. The
L114@P8: SAPs required the following
L115@P8: • liberalization of prices and marketing systems
L116@P8: • financial policy reforms
L117@P8: • international trade reforms
L118@P8: • government budget rationalization
L119@P8: • divestiture and privatization
L120@P8: • parastatals reforms, and
L121@P8: • civil service reforms.
L122@P8: The early 1980s were marked by government reluctance to implement the SAPs. By 1985
L123@P8: the shortcomings of import substitution policy were evident leading to enactment of Session
L124@P8: paper No. 1 of 1986 on Economic Management for Renewed Growth. This marked the
L125@P8: turning point of economic policy from protectionism and import substitution policies to
L126@P8-9: export promotion. This committed the government to movement away from import controls, vi
L127@P9: gradual reduction in tariffs, tariff harmonization and tariffication of quantitative restrictions.
L128@P9: The system of export incentives encouraged included Manufacturing under Bond (MUB),
L129@P9: general import duty and VAT exemption scheme, government financed export credit
L130@P9: guarantees and a proposal of a fully functioning Preferential Trade Area (PTA)
L131@P9: Kenya was classified as an ‘open’ economy in 1993 by the World Bank (WB). The first
L132@P9: wave of liberalization though not successful culminated into the second wave in which new
L133@P9: export promotion strategies were instituted and unsuccessful ones abandoned. The whole
L134@P9: period of SAPs implementation occurred at the backdrop of limited democratic space. The
L135@P9: government implemented the reforms with little or no consultation from the private sector or
L136@P9: stakeholders. The timing and sequencing of the reforms varied and the pace of
L137@P9: implementation was uneven resulting into periods of progress and stagnation. The full
L138@P9: opening of the economy in 1993 coincided with a rapidly changing political scene,
L139@P9: embracing multiparty democracy and reduced external aid. Nevertheless, economic growth
L140@P9: continued albeit in a declining manner. In 1995 the country signed onto the World Trade
L141@P9: Organization agreement (WTO) in accession to General Agreement on Trade and Tariffs
L142@P9: (GATT). Other pacts which followed later were the Common Market for Eastern and
L143@P9: Southern Africa (COMESA), African Growth Opportunity Act (AGOA) and the reviewed
L144@P9: African Caribbean Pacific-European Union (ACP-EU).
L145@P9: The onset of the liberalization saw the economy grow at a slow pace with real GDP and real
L146@P9: agricultural GDP (AgGDP) growth rates declining. Real GDP grew from 32 billion in 1980
L147@P9: to 84 billion in 1990 and 103 billion in 2000. The annual average growth rate was estimated
L148@P9: to be 17% and 2.2% in 1980/90 and 1990/00 respectively The country’s’ real GDP in 2005
L149@P9: was estimated at 1172.1 million Kshs signifying a 5.8% increase from 2004 (CBS,
L150@P9: 2006).The real GDP has been on increase since independence.. The later decline in
L151@P9: economic growth is attributed to the opening up of the economy which saw the exposure to
L152@P9: external competition.
L153@P9: Agriculture and rural development
L154@P9: Economic growth in Kenya is still strongly pegged on agricultural contribution with an
L155@P9: estimated 1% growth in agriculture resulting to 1.6% growth in the overall GDP. Despite
L156@P9: this role, over the last two decades agricultural contribution to overall economic growth has
L157@P9: declined from 34.18% in 1980, 28.38% in 1992 and finally 26.4% in 2003. The sector
L158@P9: declined from a growth of 4.4% in 1996 to 1.5% in 1999 to a negative of 2.4% in 2002 but
L159@P9: showed a minimal growth of 0.7% in 2003.
L160@P9: Agricultural and rural development cannot be divorced. Agriculture is the major livelihood
L161@P9: source to the rural households in Kenya. About 80% of Kenya’s population live in the rural
L162@P9: areas and derive their livelihood from agriculture through crop and livestock production,
L163@P9: forestry and exploitation of other natural resources. This population constitute over 70% of
L164@P9: the Kenyan population living below poverty line. The decline in agricultural growth
L165@P9: coincides with economic liberalization that opened the economy agricultural imports and
L166@P9: decline in governmental support to agriculture. Reduced government support led to increase
L167@P9: in production costs which reduced the country’s comparative advantage by making the
L168@P9: country’s agricultural products relatively more expensive.
L169@P9: The effects of liberalization have been varied on different sub-sectors and economic agents.
L170@P9-10: While some sectors of the economy have been casualties others have managed to perform vii
L171@P10: well under similar economic conditions. Sub-sectors such as coffee and sugar have
L172@P10: retrogressed under liberalization with declining crop area, output, productivities and low
L173@P10: world prices. Despite the general increase in area under coffee, production fell from a high
L174@P10: of 128.3 thousand tonnes in 1988 to a low of 45.2 thousand tonnes in 2005 while unroasted
L175@P10: coffee exports declined from 126,498 tonnes in 1986 to as low as 50,951 tonnes in 2005.
L176@P10: The poor trend in coffee exports is owed to the decline in world coffee prices. In contrast tea
L177@P10: and horticulture have had an increase in area, output, productivity, and international returns
L178@P10: during this period. The sector export value was estimated at 42.29 billion in 2005 up from
L179@P10: 22.8 billion in 1997. The area and output in tea had also increased significantly. Tea sector
L180@P10: development is mainly attributed to the KTDA which has been in the forefront of fostering
L181@P10: small-holder tea growth and the efforts of TRF which has fostered research on high yielding
L182@P10: tea varieties. On the other hand the development of the private sector dominated
L183@P10: horticultural sector is attributed to little government interference. The sector exports were
L184@P10: estimated at 13.75 billion in 1997 and 44.56 in 2005. Despite the setting of quality and
L185@P10: safety standards in the EU market, the sector has performed well.
L186@P10: Informal (off-farm) sector
L187@P10: The exit option for the declining agricultural performance over the period has been the
L188@P10: informal sector. The sector has developed since 1992 and has employed an estimated 6.12
L189@P10: million directly and indirectly. This accounts for nearly a third of countries labour force.
L190@P10: Most of the sector is rural based and with trade, service provision and manufacturing being
L191@P10: significant sub-sectors. The springing up of the informal sector is largely attributed to the
L192@P10: liberalisation of the communications sector and the cotton industry. The AGOA pact
L193@P10: although attributed for the cotton industry losses, is also recognised as having created many
L194@P10: jobs through sale of imported second hand clothes. Communications sector liberalisation
L195@P10: opened the gates to the new communication technologies and service delivery.
L196@P10: Migration, urban concentration and rural depopulation
L197@P10: Both domestic and international migrations have taken place. Domestic migration has been
L198@P10: mostly witnessed as rural-urban migration. Urban centres have increased simultaneously
L199@P10: with the increase in urban populations. Urban population reached an estimated 5.4 million in
L200@P10: 1999 up from 0.7 million in 1962. Urban centres increased from 34 in 1980 to 380 in 1999.
L201@P10: The rate of rural-urban migration when taken as the rate of growth of annual successive
L202@P10: urban population, sometimes surpassed the rural population growth rate hence resulting to
L203@P10: rural depopulation and urban concentration. The rural urban migration has deprived the rural
L204@P10: areas of agricultural labour force hence leaving rural areas with less skilled labour as it is
L205@P10: hypothesized that the educated, skilled and productive are more inclined to migration.
L206@P10: However, currently rural-urban migration trend is negative indicating that, either urban
L207@P10: households are migrating back to the rural areas or the rural households have stopped
L208@P10: migration to the urban areas. International migration declined since pre-independence period
L209@P10: to the early 1990 when it was triggered by political agitation for multi-party system. This
L210@P10: trend persisted to mid 1990s when it reversed. In 2005, estimates of international migrations
L211@P10: indicate that majority of the Kenyans are in UK which accounted for 33%, and surprisingly
L212@P10: Tanzania which accounted for 26% ahead of USA and the Southern America countries
L213@P10: which accounted for 19%. The reasons of this observed international migration pattern is
L214@P10: probably pegged on the stringency of entry rules and the anticipated job or educational
L215@P10-11: opportunities. viii
L216@P11: Remittances
L217@P11: It is evident that migrants do not sever their relationship with rural areas. The transfer of
L218@P11: funds between urban and rural inhabitants has been enhanced by reforms in the financial and
L219@P11: communications services. Although, little information on the amounts remitted and it’s
L220@P11: specific uses is available, evidence exists to show the increasing importance of remittances
L221@P11: in agriculture and rural development. Currently the Kenyan government has started to
L222@P11: recognize the significance of remittances from abroad and is assessing the possibility of
L223@P11: these remittances being substitute to Foreign Direct Investment (FDI) by creating an
L224@P11: investment environment. While there may be limitation to international data, domestic
L225@P11: remittances have been shown to increase by 1.98% from 1997to 2004
L226@P11: Trade
L227@P11: Kenya has been importing more than its exports even in the import substitution period. The
L228@P11: trend of imports and exports became more pronounced after the economy was opened up
L229@P11: and the reforms had taken place. Exchange rate fluctuations also became more pronounced
L230@P11: and dictated the import-export trends. Trade liberalization led to increase in trade volume
L231@P11: with higher imports than exports. The cover ratio declined over the period indicating
L232@P11: increasing imports. On the same trend agricultural imports are showing increase relative to
L233@P11: exports indicating the declining competitive strength of agricultural production. The extent
L234@P11: of these effects to the country’s comparative advantage however remains unknown.
L235@P11: Poverty and inequality
L236@P11: Under the economic reforms regime poverty prevalence has increased. Poverty increased
L237@P11: from 48.8% in 1981/82 to 59.56% 2000 in within the rural areas, 29.3 in 1992 to 51.48 in
L238@P11: 2000 and nationally it increased from 46.8 in 1981/82 to 56.78 in 2000. Economic reforms
L239@P11: are clearly linked to poverty under the assumption that trade reforms reduce poverty and
L240@P11: enhance human development through fostered economic growth. Trade reforms influence
L241@P11: the enterprises, distribution channels and government expenditure thereby affecting poor
L242@P11: households. The poor in Kenya are mainly found in rural and marginal areas where
L243@P11: agriculture is the main livelihood source. Arable land resource is declining owing to the
L244@P11: exploding population, labour force migration is leaving the rural areas depopulated and
L245@P11: without human capital resource. Environmental degradation is pushing resources further
L246@P11: away from the poor households reach trapping them in the environmental-poverty
L247@P11: convolution. Decline in productivity occurred at the milieu of increasing population
L248@P11: translating into increasing unemployment and consequently increasing poverty levels.
L249@P11: Reduced revenue in agricultural sector especially in sugar and coffee planting areas has
L250@P11: resulted into loss of livelihoods. It is evident that between 1997 and 2004 household income
L251@P11: declined by 6%. The disparity in income changes witnessed in various agro-ecological zones
L252@P11: is an indicator of the extent of income inequality. Further, the inequality gap has widened
L253@P11: over the years as indicated by Gini coefficient of 0.52 in 1997 and 0.55 in 2000
L254@P11: Structure and evolution of agricultural marketing chains
L255@P11: Agricultural marketing in Kenya was dominated by boards which were responsible for
L256@P11: controlling production and marketing of the output. Some of the boards were: Kenya Dairy
L257@P11-12: Board (KDB) |Formed 1958, Maize and Produce Board 1950, later evolved to the National ix
L258@P12: Cereals and Produce Board, Tea Board 1951 and the Coffee Marketing Board 1946. Upon
L259@P12: liberalization the roles and functions of these boards were refocused to make them more
L260@P12: efficient and competitive in service delivery. The liberalization of the market saw the entry
L261@P12: and exit of marketing agents with concentration and integration along the chain taking place.
L262@P12: The dairy sector, maize, coffee and tea sub-sectors witnessed the entry of new market agents
L263@P12: while those incompetent withdrew from the market. The boards partly maintained regulatory
L264@P12: roles mainly to maintain standards and quality of produce. There was also emergence of
L265@P12: alternative marketing chains especially in maize and dairy were it is estimated that these
L266@P12: alternative chains control a significant share of the market. For instance, in dairy it is
L267@P12: estimated that the informal chain control over 80% of the marketed milk.
L268@P12: Input supply chains such as those of maize seed and fertilizers were also liberalized.
L269@P12: Increased use of these inputs is attributed to the extensive availability which was achieved
L270@P12: after freeing the market.
L271@P12: Segmentation in production structures
L272@P12: Agricultural production in Kenya is characteristically regional. The country is segmented on
L273@P12: the basis of climatic conditions each supporting a major agricultural production.
L274@P12: Development segmentation was started during the colonial. This was perpetuated by post
L275@P12: independence governments by that emphasised the development of the high potential regions
L276@P12: at the expense of the rural arid areas. Economic reforms initiated in the early 1980s further
L277@P12: segmented even the high potential areas triggering within region segmentation. Today,
L278@P12: segmentation can be viewed in terms of agricultural production that is dictated by climatic
L279@P12: conditions and public investment in infrastructue. Economic reforms has had different
L280@P12: effects in different regions depending on the type of agricultural production in the area and
L281@P12: resulting in economic imbalances, poverty and inequity.
L282@P12: Liberalization appears to have reinforced differential development that was initiated during
L283@P12: the colonial period through classification of regions on the basis of crop potential and
L284@P12: subsequent resource allocation. Liberalization effects on large and small-scale farmers does
L285@P12: not present a clear picture but can be discerned more clearly along commodity lines.
L286@P12: While this study has identified structural changes that have taken place in the rural setting
L287@P12: under the pre-liberalization, liberalization and post-liberalization eras, the analytical level
L288@P12: cannot ascribe liberalization per se. Whereas it is feasible to match policy changes with
L289@P12: changes in economic variables, the causality remains an empirical issue.
L290@P12: Manifestations of economic reforms and reshaping of the
L291@P12: rural economies
L292@P12: While the vulnerable groups are these losing their livelihoods due to economic reforms, they
L293@P12: are distributed within the country irrespective of the regions production potential. These are
L294@P12: characterised by declining agricultural revenues that leads to declining education and health
L295@P12: levels, declining food security and increasing poverty. It is shown that different regions
L296@P12: exhibit varying levels of poverty that is consistent with the loss in agricultural income of the
L297@P12-13: main agricultural products especially export crops.x
L298@P13: The government has initiated several programs and policies to reverse these trends. These
L299@P13: efforts include The Poverty Reduction Strategy Paper 2001 (PRSP) and the Economic
L300@P13: Recovery Strategy for Wealth and Employment Creation (2003) that spells out strategies for
L301@P13: rural economy reconstruction focusing on sectors that have a direct impact on the rural
L302@P13: economy through industrialization, employment creation and decentralization of social
L303@P13: infrastructure and services. Other strategies include The Ministry of Livestock and Fisheries
L304@P13: Development Strategic Plan 2003 –2007, Strategy for Revitalizing Agriculture, 2004 – 2014,

L307@P13: This study was carried to elicit the structural implications of economic liberalization on
L308@P13: agriculture and rural development. Several outcomes and challenges were identified aside
L309@P13: from tracing agricultural policy evolution.
L310@P13: While economic growth declined, population increased over the reform period. Agricultural
L311@P13: performance deteriorated and similarly did the household incomes as population increase
L312@P13: continued to exert pressure on natural resources. Increasing urbanisation, internal and
L313@P13: external migrations and the corresponding rural depopulation and human capital depletion
L314@P13: pose new challenges.
L315@P13: Liberalization of markets has stimulated the interest and encouraged entry of multiple
L316@P13: players in the many channels. Whereas this is seen as enhancing market efficiency, the
L317@P13: problem of enforcing food safety and maintenance of quality standards is creeping into the
L318@P13: system. Exit of players from the channels has led to concentration in the market and requires
L319@P13: the development of contract farming and adherence to business ethics. Structures for
L320@P13: competitive and ethical behaviour of economic agents have to be developed.
L321@P13: Segmentation initiated during the settler period has been perpetuated by the reforms. This
L322@P13: can be witnessed in view of agricultural production that is shaped by regional climatic
L323@P13: conditions and public infrastructure investments. New livelihood strategies, especially the
L324@P13: non-farm activities have emerged due to declining farm revenue emanating from declining
L325@P13: land and labour productivity, farm sizes and arable land per capita.
L326@P13: Vulnerable groups are distributed all over the country and these livelihoods are at stake due
L327@P13: to the economic changes. Loss of these livelihoods is perpetuated by decline in agricultural
L328@P13: income triggered by increasing input prices and declining per unit output. This has forced
L329@P13: rural households to venture into alternative agricultural and non-agricultural enterprises.
L330@P13: Rural economies are currently reshaping under the thrust of migration, remittances and the
L331@P13: development of non-farm activities with the new configuration being dictated by
L332@P13: government expenditure on agriculture and investment choices for domestic and
L333@P13-14: international remittances. 1
L334@P14: INTRODUCTION
L335@P14: The evolution of economic policies in Kenya dates back to the colonial era (period preceding 1954)
L336@P14: that was characterized by settler targeted government assistance and exclusion of indigenous
L337@P14: populations from commercial agriculture. This was followed by the period spanning 1955-62 under
L338@P14: which the Swynnerton Plan was implemented. This plan involved the first land reforms and opened
L339@P14: up commercial farming to the indigenous Kenyan farmers. The period 1963 to 1982 or the early
L340@P14: independence period saw the emergence of small-holder farmers and heavy government involvement
L341@P14: resulting in the rapid growth of agriculture and rural sector. The liberalization era come into being in
L342@P14: 1982 and continued to 2002. This was characterized by government withdrawal from service
L343@P14: provision, deregulation of markets and increased private sector involvement. The post liberalization
L344@P14: era began in 2003 under which the Economic Recovery Strategy for Wealth and Employment
L345@P14: Creation (ERS-WEC), Strategy for Revitalizing Agriculture (SRA) are being implemented
L346@P14: The post-independence economic experience in Kenya can be categorised into three distinct stages:
L347@P14: • The pre-liberalization era (period before 1980)
L348@P14: • The liberalization era (1980-2002)
L349@P14: • The post-liberalization era (2003 and the period after)
L350@P14: The liberalization era can further be viewed in two stages: the early 80’s to early 90’s when the
