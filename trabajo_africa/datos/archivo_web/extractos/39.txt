L164@P6: 1. EXECUTIVE SUMMARY
L165@P6: Kenya’s affordable housing ambition is supported by strong policy momentum, a growing formal workforce, and
L166@P6: an expanding pool of long-term domestic savings through pension schemes. Yet despite this enabling
L167@P6: environment, homeownership remains out of reach for many formally employed households. This report
L168@P6: examines the structural affordability gap that persists even among pension contributors and evaluates how
L169@P6: Kenya’s pension system can support housing access in a manner that safeguards retirement security and remains
L170@P6: consistent with existing policy and regulatory frameworks.
L171@P6: The report finds that the core constraint is not housing demand, nor the availability of savings capital, but the
L172@P6: mismatch between housing prices, financing structures, and real household income patterns. Analysis of
L173@P6: affordability conditions shows that effective affordability is concentrated within a narrow price band, typically
L174@P6: between KShs 1.7 million and KShs 3.4 million for many middle-income households, while much of formal
L175@P6: housing supply remains priced above this range. Where supply does exist within this price band, it is largely
L176@P6: limited to very small bedsits and studio units, which do not fit the actual household needs of most owneroccupiers, particularly families requiring multi-room housing and adequate living space. High deposit
L177@P6: requirements, elevated interest rates, and rigid repayment terms further limit the ability of households to
L178@P6: translate housing aspirations into bankable housing finance demand.
L179@P6: As a result, rental housing continues to be more affordable and more responsive to household income realities.
L180@P6: Rental payments typically require significantly lower upfront costs than homeownership, involve shorter-term
L181@P6: commitments, and provide greater flexibility in managing income volatility, employment mobility, and changing
L182@P6: household size. For many households, renting therefore remains the economically rational choice, reinforcing a
L183@P6: structural disconnect between housing supply, financing mechanisms, and effective owner-occupier demand
L184@P6: despite sustained demand for housing in general.
L185@P6: Zamara pension member data reinforces this affordability challenge. While most members may be eligible to
L186@P6: apply for housing loans based on formal employment status, affordability remains constrained by income levels
L187@P6: and competing household priorities. A significant majority of members earn below KShs 150,000 per month, and
L188@P6: many carry existing debt obligations that reduce their available debt-service capacity for housing finance. This
L189@P6: affordability compression is reflected in low mortgage penetration even among formally employed contributors,
L190@P6: and a strong preference for alternative pathways to homeownership. Rental housing therefore remains the
L191@P6: primary tenure choice for many members, while alternative pathways such as incremental construction,
L192@P6: cooperative housing arrangements, employer-supported schemes, and pension-linked financing provide more
L193@P6: viable routes to ownership that better align with income progression and long-term savings behaviour.
L194@P6: The report further confirms that incremental housing is not a secondary option, but the dominant and rational
L195@P6: pathway to homeownership for most households. Many households prefer to build progressively, aligning
L196@P6: housing investment decisions to irregular cash flows, lifecycle needs, and risk management concerns. Survey
L197@P6: evidence highlights demand for smaller, staged financing tranches linked to construction milestones rather than
L198@P6: large, one-time mortgage products. This behavioural reality creates a clear product design gap in Kenya’s housing
L199@P6: finance market, where traditional mortgages are structured around fully completed units and fixed repayment
L200@P6: schedules.
L201@P6: Kenya’s pension system has substantial capacity, with total assets approaching KShs 2.5 trillion, and a regulatory
L202@P6: framework that permits both demand-side and supply-side participation in housing. On the demand side, the
L203@P6: Retirement Benefits (Mortgage Loans) Regulations, published in the Kenya Gazette on 12 June 2009 and
L204@P6: commenced on the same date enable pension-backed loan guarantees through assignment of accrued benefits
L205@P6: as collateral. On the supply side, pension schemes are permitted to invest in property, REITs, and
L206@P6: infrastructure-related assets within prescribed investment limits. However, utilisation remains minimal due to
L207@P6: limited awareness, operational complexity, lender risk appetite, and most critically, the underlying affordability
L208@P6: mismatch between available housing stock and member income bands.
L209@P6: Global experience demonstrates that pension-linked housing mechanisms can be effective, but only when system
L210@P6: design is deliberate and aligned to household affordability and market supply. Successful models show that
L211@P6: pensions are most effective when used to reduce lender risk and unlock financing, rather than substituting

L239@P8: 2. INTRODUCTION
L240@P8: Zamara Group Limited, in partnership with the Centre for Affordable Housing Finance in Africa (CAHF) and
L241@P8: Financial Sector Deepening (FSD) Kenya, undertook this study to address a critical and persistent challenge in
L242@P8: Kenya’s housing market: why formally employed households with stable incomes and consistent pension
L243@P8: contributions remain largely excluded from homeownership.
L244@P8: Conventional housing policy and finance narratives often assume that formal employment equates to housing
L245@P8: affordability. However, emerging evidence suggests that this assumption no longer holds. Despite regular
L246@P8: incomes and disciplined long-term saving through pension schemes, many formally employed Kenyans are
L247@P8: unable to access housing finance that aligns with their income realities, savings patterns, existing debt
L248@P8: obligations, and housing pathways. High levels of consumer and short-term credit further compress disposable
L249@P8: income and debt-service capacity, weakening the link between formal financial inclusion and effective housing
L250@P8: finance access. This disconnect has resulted in a growing cohort of households that are financially included in
L251@P8: theory but effectively excluded from homeownership in practice.
L252@P8: This study explores whether Kenya’s pension system, by virtue of its scale, structure, and long-term orientation,
L253@P8: can play a complementary role in addressing this gap. Specifically, it examines whether pension savings can
L254@P8: support housing affordability in ways that expand access without compromising retirement security, rather than
L255@P8: attempting to replace traditional housing finance mechanisms.
L256@P8: The analysis draws on demand-side evidence from nearly 150,000 active pension scheme members under
L257@P8: Zamara’s administration. This dataset enables a detailed understanding of income stability, savings behaviour,
L258@P8: housing aspirations, and affordability constraints among formally employed households, an often-overlooked
L259@P8: segment that sits between social housing eligibility and conventional mortgage affordability.
L260@P8: The study is anchored in the recognition that affordability, rather than willingness to save, is the binding
L261@P8: constraint for most households. While pension participation demonstrates strong financial discipline, existing
L262@P8: housing finance products remain poorly aligned with household income levels, prevailing debt obligations, and
L263@P8: the incremental ways in which most Kenyans acquire or build homes over time. As a result, regulatory provisions
L264@P8: that permit pension-linked housing finance have not translated into meaningful uptake or impact at scale
L265@P8: Addressing this gap requires more than product-level adjustments; it calls for ecosystem-level reform. On the
L266@P8: supply side, the persistent mismatch between income bands and available housing stock reflects structural
L267@P8: constraints — high land costs in urban areas, infrastructure deficits that inflate development costs, lengthy
L268@P8: approval processes, taxation layers, and limited access to patient development capital. Without coordinated
L269@P8: interventions to reduce the cost of serviced land, streamline approvals, incentivize affordable housing
L270@P8: construction, and deepen capital markets for long-term housing finance, affordability will remain elusive
L271@P8: regardless of pension participation levels.
L272@P8: The Kenya Mortgage Refinance Company (KMRC) product design further illustrates the structural tension. While
L273@P8: KMRC has expanded access to longer-tenor funding for primary lenders, its Build Product caps the maximum
L274@P8: loan at KShs 10.5 million and the maximum property value (inclusive of the loan) at KShs 15 million. In practice,
L275@P8: these thresholds exclude a significant segment of formally employed households in peri-urban and urban areas
L276@P8: where land values alone often approach or exceed these limits. For many middle-income earners, especially
L277@P8: those who already own land acquired years earlier at appreciating values, the combined land and construction
L278@P8: cost surpasses the eligibility ceiling effectively disqualifying them despite stable incomes and pension savings.
L279@P8: Moreover, housing acquisition in Kenya is rarely a single-transaction mortgage event. It is typically incremental
L280@P8: land purchase first, followed by phased construction financed through savings, SACCO loans, informal credit, or
L281@P8: employer advances. A product structure anchored on a capped, fully underwritten mortgage model does not
L282@P8: fully align with this pathway. The result is a paradox: households that are financially disciplined, pension-active,
L283@P8: and formally employed remain constrained not by willingness or even by total lifetime earning potential, but by
L284@P8: rigid affordability metrics and product thresholds that fail to reflect market land values and incremental building
L285@P8: realities.
L286@P8: Closing this gap will require coordinated policy alignment across regulators, lenders, developers, pension
L287@P8: trustees, and infrastructure agencies — including re-examining loan caps in relation to market dynamics,
L288@P8: incentivizing lower-cost housing typologies, promoting serviced land development, expanding blended finance
L289@P8-9: models, and designing phased or hybrid mortgage products that better reflect how Kenyans actually build. Affordable housing access for Kenya’s formal workforce:

L306@P10: 3. STUDY SCOPE AND OBJECTIVES
L307@P10: 3.1. Problem Statement
L308@P10: Kenya’s affordable housing challenge persists despite strong household demand, ongoing policy interventions,
L309@P10: and rising pools of long-term savings through pension systems. While formal-sector workers contribute
L310@P10: consistently to retirement schemes, conventional housing finance products, particularly mortgage loans, remain
L311@P10: inaccessible to most households due to deposit requirements, loan pricing, repayment constraints, and the
L312@P10: misalignment between housing supply prices and effective household affordability. For many households, high
L313@P10: debt-to-income ratios driven by personal loans, SACCO facilities, mobile credit, and other short-term borrowing
L314@P10: further compress mortgage eligibility, even where incomes are stable and pension savings are consistent.
L315@P10: Rental housing continues to be more attainable and affordable as a tenure for most households driven by
L316@P10: structural differences in supply requirements, financing and affordability. For example, building built for rental
L317@P10: by the owner face more regulatory flexibility (less stringent parking requirements, fire and infrastructure
L318@P10: standards compared to formally financed for sale developments) and lower financing costs as financed by the
L319@P10: owner’s equity.
L320@P10: This study examines whether, and how, pension savings and pension capital can play a practical role in expanding
L321@P10: access to affordable housing in Kenya without undermining retirement income security.
L322@P10: 3.2. Study Objectives
L323@P10: The objectives of the study are to:
L324@P10: 1. Quantify housing affordability among formally employed pension members using observed contribution
L325@P10: and pension balance data rather than proxy assumptions.
L326@P10: 2. Assess feasibility of housing finance options, including long-term mortgages and incremental housing
L327@P10: finance pathways, under realistic prudential constraints.
