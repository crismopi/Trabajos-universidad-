L28@P3: iii
L29@P3: The role of the Central Bank of Kenya (CBK) is anchored in Section 231 of Kenya’s Constitution and in the CBK Act.
L30@P3: The CBK is responsible for formulating monetary policy to achieve and maintain price stability and issuing currency.
L31@P3: The Bank also promotes financial stability through regulation, supervision, and licensing of financial institutions
L32@P3: under its mandate. It also provides oversight of the payments, clearing and settlement systems, and fosters liquidity,
L33@P3: solvency, and proper functioning of the financial system. The CBK formulates and implements the foreign exchange
L34@P3: policy and manages foreign exchange reserves. It is also the banker for, adviser to, and fiscal agent of the Government.
L35@P3: The CBK’s monetary policy is designed to support the Government’s objectives with respect to growth. The CBK
L36@P3: formulates and conducts monetary policy with the aim of keeping overall inflation within the target prescribed by the
L37@P3: National Treasury at the beginning of the financial year. Currently, this target is 5 percent with a range between 2.5
L38@P3: percent and 7.5 percent.
L39@P3: The achievement and maintenance of a low and stable inflation rate coupled with adequate liquidity in the market,
L40@P3: facilitates higher levels of domestic savings and private investment. This leads to improved economic growth, higher
L41@P3: real incomes, and increased employment opportunities.
L42@P3: The Principal Objectives of the Central Bank of Kenya
L43@P3: Instruments and Transmission of Monetary Policy
L44@P3: The CBK pursues its monetary policy objectives using the following instruments:
L45@P3: • Open Market Operations (OMO): This refers to actions by the CBK involving purchases and sales of eligible
L46@P3: securities to regulate the money supply and the credit conditions in the economy. OMO can also be used to
L47@P3: stabilize short-term interest rates. When the Central Bank buys securities on the open market, it increases the
L48@P3: reserves of commercial banks, making it possible for them to expand their loans and hence increase the money
L49@P3: supply. To achieve the desired level of money supply, OMO is conducted using:
L50@P3: i. Repurchase Agreements (Repos): A repo is a collateralized loan involving a contractual arrangement
L51@P3: between two parties, in which one party sells a security at a specified price with a commitment to buy the
L52@P3: security back at a later date. Both parties therefore, meet their investment goals of secured funding and
L53@P3: liquidity. CBK Repos are conducted through auctions with tenors of 3 and 7 days and are for mopping up
L54@P3: liquidity from the market. The Late Repo, sold in the afternoon, has a 4-day tenor and is issued at 100 basis
L55@P3: points below the repo rate of the day. Reverse Repos, on the other hand, are for liquidity injections and involve
L56@P3: purchase of securities from commercial banks. The current tenors for Reverse Repos are 7, 14, 21, 28 and
L57@P3: 91 days.
L58@P3: ii. Term Auction Deposit (TAD): The TAD is used when the securities held by the CBK for Repo purposes are
L59@P3: exhausted or when CBK considers it desirable to offer longer dated tenors. TAD is essentially not backed by
L60@P3: collateral, and it is conducted through an auction, similar to Repos. Currently, the tenors for such deposits
L61@P3: at CBK are 1, 7, 14, 21, 28 or 91 days and upon maturity of TAD, the CBK credits the respective commercial
L62@P3-4: bank with the deposit and interest. CENTRAL BANK OF KENYA

L64@P4: iv
L65@P4: iii. Horizontal Repos: Horizontal Repos are modes of improving liquidity distribution between commercial
L66@P4: banks and are conducted under CBK supervision. They are transacted between commercial banks on the
L67@P4: basis of signed agreements using government securities as collateral and have negotiated tenors and yields.
L68@P4: Commercial banks, short of deposits at the CBK, borrow from banks with excess deposits on the security
L69@P4: of an appropriate asset, normally a government security. Horizontal Repos also help banks overcome the
L70@P4: problem of limits to lines of credit, thus promoting more efficient management of interbank liquidity.
L71@P4: • Central Bank Rate (CBR): The CBR is reviewed and announced by the Monetary Policy Committee (MPC) at
L72@P4: least every two months. Movements in the CBR, both in direction and magnitude, signal the monetary policy
L73@P4: stance. To enhance monetary policy transmission the MPC adopted a new monetary policy implementation
L74@P4: framework in August 2023. The framework is based on inflation targeting and on interest rate corridor around the
L75@P4: CBR, currently at 50 basis points. Consistent with the new framework, monetary policy operations are aimed at
L76@P4: ensuring the Kenya Shilling Overnight Interbank Average (KESONIA) as an operating target closely align with the
L77@P4: CBR.
L78@P4: The efficiency in the Repo and interbank markets is crucial for the transmission of monetary policy decisions. The
L79@P4: CBK monitors the overnight interbank money market. It responds to the tightness or slackness in the interbank
L80@P4: market liquidity through OMO. Short-term international flows of capital are affected by short-term interest rates
L81@P4: in the country. These are, in turn, affected by movements in the CBR and hence indirectly, the exchange rate
L82@P4: could also be affected.
L83@P4: • Standing Facilities: The CBK does not have automatic standing facilities with respect to overnight lending. The
L84@P4: CBK, as lender of last resort, provides secured loans to commercial banks on an overnight basis at a penal rate
L85@P4: that is over the CBR. This facility is referred to as the Discount Window. To improve access to the discount window
L86@P4: the applicable interest rate is 50 basis points above CBR (upper bound of the interest rate corridor).
L87@P4: • The Cash Reserves Ratio (CRR): In accordance with the law, the CRR is the proportion of a commercial bank’s
L88@P4: total deposit liabilities which must be held as deposits at CBK. These deposits are held in the CRR Account at
L89@P4: no interest. During the review period, the ratio was lowered in February 2025 from 4.25 percent to 3.25 percent
L90@P4: of the total of a bank’s domestic and foreign currency deposit liabilities. To facilitate commercial banks’ liquidity
L91@P4: management, commercial banks are required to maintain their CRR based on a daily average level from the 15th
L92@P4: of the previous month to the 14th of the current month and not to fall below a CRR of 2 percent on any day.
L93@P4: • Licensing and Supervision of Financial Institutions: The CBK uses the licensing and supervision tools to
L94@P4: ensure stability and efficiency of the banking system; this includes vetting potential managers for suitability.
L95@P4: • The National Payments System: The modernisation of the National Payments System has continued to lower
L96@P4: transaction costs and enhanced the efficiency of the payments systems. This has ensured the effectiveness of
L97@P4: monetary policy instruments.
L98@P4: • Communication: The increasing use of communication media ensures a wider dissemination of monetary policy
L99@P4: decisions and background data thereby increasing the efficiency of information transmission and managing
L100@P4: expectations. The regular interaction between the MPC and the Chief Executive Officers of banks has ensured
L101@P4: that monetary policy decisions are transmitted to the banking sector. The regular Governor’s Press Conferences
L102@P4: have also enhanced the media understanding of monetary policy decisions. The CBK website is an important
L103@P4: source of up-to date data on all aspects of the financial market including interest rates, exchange rates, results of
L104@P4-5: auctions of government securities, and the MPC releases.CENTRAL BANK OF KENYA

L107@P5: This Monetary Policy Statement provides the direction of monetary policy in the financial year 2026/27, and reviews
L108@P5: the outcomes of the policy measures implemented in the first half of 2026.
L109@P5: During the first half of 2026, the Central Bank of Kenya (CBK) conducted monetary policy with the objective of
L110@P5: maintaining overall inflation within the target range of 5±2.5 percent and supporting sustainable economic growth.
L111@P5: Monetary policy was implemented against a backdrop of moderating global growth, heightened geopolitical tensions,
L112@P5: particularly the conflict in the Middle East, and elevated trade policy uncertainty. The conflict disrupted global supply
L113@P5: chains and contributed to higher energy prices and inflationary pressures globally. Inflation in major economies
L114@P5: remained above target levels, reflecting persistent core inflation and rising energy costs, prompting central banks to
L115@P5: adopt a cautious approach to monetary policy. While global growth continued to be supported by technology-driven
L116@P5: investment and resilient demand in some economies, risks to the outlook remained elevated due to geopolitical
L117@P5: tensions, trade fragmentation, and global economic uncertainty.
L118@P5: The Monetary Policy Committee (MPC) held three meetings during the first half of 2026. In February 2026, the MPC
L119@P5: lowered the Central Bank Rate (CBR) by 25 basis points to 8.75 percent from 9.00 percent to support private sector
L120@P5: lending and economic activity while maintaining price and exchange rate stability. The MPC maintained the CBR at
L121@P5: 8.75 percent in both April and June 2026 meetings, in order to anchor inflation expectations arising from higher energy
L122@P5: prices attributed to the conflict in the Middle East.
L123@P5: Kenya’s overall inflation remained within the target range of 5±2.5 percent between December 2025 and June 2026.
L124@P5: Inflation rose to 6.4 percent in June 2026 from 4.5 percent in December 2025. Non-core inflation increased to 15.1
L125@P5: percent from 11.2 percent over the same period, mainly due to higher domestic energy prices, following increases in
L126@P5: international energy prices. Core inflation also increased to 3.1 percent in June 2026 from 2.0 percent in December
L127@P5: 2025, largely reflecting higher transport costs associated with elevated energy prices.
L128@P5: The foreign exchange market remained stable in the first half of 2026 despite heightened global uncertainty. This
L129@P5: stability was supported by diversified inflows, including diaspora remittances, horticulture export earnings, and travel
L130@P5: receipts as well as ample reverses. The current account deficit was estimated at 3.0 percent of GDP in the 12 months
L131@P5: to June 2026, compared with 1.9 percent in a similar period in 2025. CBK foreign exchange reserves stood at USD
L132@P5: 14,023 million in June 2026, equivalent to 5.8 months of import cover, and continued to provide an adequate buffer
L133@P5: against short-term foreign exchange market shocks.
L134@P5: The banking sector remained stable and resilient in the first half of 2026, supported by strong liquidity and capital
L135@P5: adequacy ratios. The average commercial banks’ liquidity and capital adequacy ratios stood at 61.2 percent and
L136@P5: 20.0 percent, respectively, in June 2026, well above the statutory limits. Credit risk eased with the ratio of gross nonperforming loans (NPLs) to gross loans declining to 14.8 percent in June 2026 from 15.4 percent in December 2025,
L137@P5: and banks continued to make adequate provisions for NPLs. Growth in private sector credit strengthened further in
L138@P5: the first half of 2026, rising to 10.6 percent in June 2026 from 5.9 percent in December 2025, reflecting improved
L139@P5: demand for credit alongside decline in lending interest rates. The Risk Based Credit Pricing Model (RBCPM), which
L140@P5: became fully operational in March 2026, continued to support the transmission of monetary policy to lending rates.
L141@P5: The weighted average lending rate declined gradually to 14.4 percent in June 2026 from 14.8 percent in December
L142@P5: 2025.
L143@P5: The growth of the economy moderated to 4.6 percent in 2025 from 4.7 percent in 2024, reflecting slower growth in the
L144@P5: agriculture and services sectors, partly due to unfavourable weather conditions and a moderation in activity across
L145@P5: several services sub-sectors. However, the economy continued to benefit from a strong recovery in industrial activity,
L146@P5: particularly in construction, mining and quarrying, and electricity and water supply. Growth strengthened in the first
L147@P5: quarter of 2026 to 5.3 percent, supported by improved performance in the industry and services sectors, notably
L148@P5: construction, manufacturing, financial and insurance activities, and real estate. This performance underscores the
L149@P5: continued resilience of the Kenyan economy despite a challenging global environment.

L153@P6: The monetary policy stance in the FY 2026/27 will aim at maintaining overall inflation within the target range of 5±2.5
L154@P6: percent and supporting the government economic growth objective. The economy is projected to grow by 4.9 percent
L155@P6: in 2026, supported by strong performance of agriculture and services sectors, as well as a recovery of the industrial
L156@P6: sector. Growth in credit to the private sector is projected to remain resilient at about 11.0 percent by December 2026,
L157@P6: underpinned by lower interest rates and sustained improvement in economic activity. The foreign exchange market
L158@P6: is expected to remain stable, supported by resilient exports receipts, and buoyant remittances. The current account
L159@P6: deficit is projected to widen to 3.0 percent of GDP in 2026, but is expected to be more than fully financed by financial
L160@P6: and capital account inflows.
L161@P6: The Bank will continue to implement reforms outlined in the White Paper on Modernization of the Monetary Policy
L162@P6: Framework and Operations, to strengthen the monetary policy framework and operations. The Bank will also continue
L163@P6: to hold regular engagements with stakeholders in the banking sector, as well as with CEOs of non-bank private sector
L164@P6: firms. Continued coordination between monetary and fiscal policy is expected to reinforce macroeconomic stability.
L165@P6: The Bank will continue to monitor the impact of its policy measures, as well as developments in the global and
L166@P6-7: domestic economy, in order to safeguard price stability.CENTRAL BANK OF KENYA

L200@P7: and relatively accommodative financial conditions
L201@P7: continue to support economic activity. In contrast,
L202@P7: many energy-importing and low-income economies
L203@P7: face weaker growth prospects due to higher commodity

L206@P7: moderate in 2026, reflecting the effects of tariffs, trade
L207@P7: fragmentation, and supply chain adjustments. The

L211@P7: further trade fragmentation, supply chain disruptions,
L212@P7: elevated public debt, tighter financing conditions, and
L213@P7: the potential correction in technology-related asset
L214@P7: valuations. On the upside, stronger-than-expected AI
L215@P7: investment, faster normalization of energy markets,
L216@P7: renewed international cooperation to reduce trade
L217@P7: barriers, and structural reforms could support stronger
L218@P7: global growth.
